The traditional view

Pressure falls along the capillary while plasma oncotic pressure stays fixed. Filtration at the arteriolar end is followed by absorption at the venular end, so most filtered fluid is predicted to return to the capillary. The opposing oncotic pressure is the plasma minus interstitial value.

The revised view

The endothelial glycocalyx is the semipermeable layer. Fluid filtered through it enters a small space beneath it that is washed by filtrate, so its protein concentration (πg) stays low. The relevant oncotic gradient is πc − πg, and the bulk interstitial value matters little while filtration is brisk.

The no-absorption rule

When filtration slows, the subglycocalyx protein concentration rises toward the interstitial value and the oncotic gradient collapses. In steady state, most capillaries filter along their whole length or approach zero flux; they do not keep absorbing. Filtrate returns to the circulation through lymph. Absorption can occur transiently (for minutes) after an abrupt fall in Pc, such as after hemorrhage.

How this lab computes it

Pc falls linearly along the capillary. In steady state, the revised model solves for flux at each point with πg,eff = πi + (πg − πi)(1 − e−Lp·NDP/2), and flux is held at zero where the traditional balance would predict absorption. Transient mode uses πg as set. Local flux is shown in nL·min⁻¹·cm⁻² and net Jv in µL/min. This is a teaching simplification, not a full Michel–Weinbaum model.
